Storm season tests the world's busiest ports as freight costs climb

Shipping lines are rerouting vessels and stacking containers inland while insurers rewrite their winter cover.

irtBy Irtnews staff24 November 2026 · 5 min read

Harbour masters along the northern shipping lanes have spent the past week doing something they rarely do in autumn: turning ships away. A run of early storms has closed berths for hours at a time, and the backlog is now measured in days rather than hours.

Freight forwarders say the cost of moving a container on the busiest routes has risen sharply since the start of the month, as carriers add surcharges for delays and longer routes around bad weather. Retailers who rely on just-in-time deliveries are already moving stock to inland depots to keep shelves full through the holiday season.

Why the queues are growing

A modern container ship can carry many thousands of boxes, and unloading one is a tightly choreographed job for cranes, trucks and rail wagons. When wind speeds pass the safe limit, the cranes stop. Every hour lost at the quay ripples out to the trucks waiting at the gate and the trains booked to leave that evening.

Port operators say they have added night shifts and opened overflow yards, but space is limited. Some terminals are asking shipping lines to collect empty containers faster so that full ones have somewhere to go.

Every hour a crane stands still is an hour that has to be found somewhere else in the week.

Who pays

Insurers are less relaxed than the ports. Several have rewritten winter policies to exclude delays caused by repeated closures, a change that shifts more of the risk onto shippers and, eventually, shoppers.

Small importers say they feel the change most. Large retailers can book space months ahead and spread cargo across several ports. A family business bringing in one or two containers a month has far less room to manoeuvre and often pays the spot price on the day.

What happens next

Forecasters expect the stormy pattern to ease in the coming weeks, which would let terminals work through the backlog. Industry groups are using the disruption to argue for more investment in sheltered berths and automated cranes that can keep working in stronger winds.

For now, the advice to businesses is simple: order early, keep a little more stock than usual, and expect deliveries to take longer than the timetable says.
